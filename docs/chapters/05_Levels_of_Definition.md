## **5. 定义的层次 (Levels of Definition)**

Hempel (1952) 将经验科学中的定义划分为三个层次：**名义定义（nominal definition）**、**含义分析（meaning analysis）** 和 **经验分析（empirical analysis）**。在自然科学研究中，这三个层次的实用性呈递进关系。然而对于传播学研究而言，含义分析才是更为核心的工作；经验定义虽是一个重要的考量因素，但若要像物理学家那样，严格地在经验层面完全剖析一个概念的完整含义，是极不可能实现的。

本大纲将遵循 Hempel 的三个定义层次。不过，读者切勿假设所有的概念阐释最终都会导向经验分析（即一个概念最终都能被简化为一个令人满意的操作化定义）。相比于自然科学，传播学研究中的定义要多样得多，且在不同的理论语境下有着截然不同的构想。即便如此，我们仍有大量的工作要做。


### **名义定义 (Nominal Definition)**

人们经常为具体的对象（如《纽约时报》、Stephen 或西尔斯大厦）以及对象的类别（如儿童、备忘录）赋予名称。这些名义标签在日常交谈中完全够用，但当应用于传播学中更为抽象的思想（如信息、理解、怯懦/沉默寡言）时，只有当标签与对象之间附带有彼此理解的连接关系时，它们才具有建设性。**名义定义是一种缺乏连接性说明的任意命名；而含义分析则恰恰提供了这种具体的连接说明。** 名义定义是作为一种约定俗成的习惯而被采纳的；经验定义则能揭示出明确的区分。例如，我们可以将《华尔街日报》视为一份报纸，是因为我们大多数人都同意它是报纸（名义定义），或者是由于它符合某些将报纸与其他事物区分开来的既定标准。

当名义定义超出其原始语境时，可能会产生问题。例如，当电子计算机器最初被制造出来时，将它们称为“计算机（computer）”显得十分自然。然而在今天，“计算机”一词已无法很好地描述这些机器在大多数时间里为我们大多数人所做的工作。对于一个正在学习“在电脑上写作”的年轻学生，我们可能不得不解释一点历史（以及关于计算机诸多用途的大量知识），以便他能够理解这个约定俗成的名称。工业词源学解释了传播学中许多如今令人困惑的概念之所以能长期存在的原因，比如来自印刷业的术语（如新闻界/印刷机 [the press]、小写字母 [lower case]）。曾经指代具体实体的词语已演变成抽象概念，其含义的生存独立于这些原始根源。这里的核心观点在于：**给某个对象或想法赋予一个名称，并不等于对该概念做出了能够将其含义延续至其他语境中的定义。**


### **含义分析 (Meaning Analysis)**

分析一个概念含义的过程可以通过两种途径来进行：**提炼法（distillation）** 和 **列举法（list）**。无论采用哪种途径，将我们的思考组织为低阶概念和高阶概念都是非常有益的。低阶概念更贴近观察世界（无论是在日常生活还是在人工设定的实验室环境中）。高阶概念包含/包容（subsume）了若干低阶概念，这种含义结构的层级组织可以被想象为一棵定义上的“概念树”。这棵树的树干是我们正在阐释的单一概念；紧接着从树干分叉出的是少数几个高阶概念；每个高阶概念可以进一步分叉为若干低阶概念；而每个低阶概念又可能萌生出一个或多个操作化定义。含义分析很大程度上是一个智力思考的过程，占据了这一结构中的树干和大枝干——同时又并未完全脱离对操作化的关注。

对概念抽象含义的**提炼**，始于阅读许多不同学者对该概念的论述。随后需要经过一个智力加工过程，将这一思想蒸馏/浓缩（boil down）为其核心要素。本质上，不同的作者在使用这个术语时究竟表达什么意思？提炼的目标可能是寻找该概念的中心含义，但这在实践中极少能够实现，因为很少有概念拥有被所有学者共同认可的单一含义。相反，此类分析的标准产物通常是**多种含义**，每种含义被一部分学者所使用。

Carter (1962) 对“刻板印象（stereotyping）”这一概念的三种不同含义的辨识，就是提炼法的一个范例。他注意到，不同的作者使用该术语分别指代态度的**极化（polarization）**、**固定化（fixedness）** 或 **同质化（homogenization）**。在他对竞选活动效果的研究中，他发现只有“同质化”与大众传播呈现出增长关系。

许多由两个词构成的概念（双词概念）都带有两种（或更多）含义，但在论述性写作中这一点往往并不明显。“社会事实/社会现实（social reality）”就是一个例子。McLeod 与 Chaffee (1972) 提炼了数十篇使用“social reality”一词（且通常没有给出明确定义）的文献。他们在这类文献中发现了两种截然不同、在某些方面甚至相反的含义，他们将其称为“社会-真实（social REALITY, $sR$）”和“社会-现实（SOCIAL reality, $Sr$）”。

* 第一种含义（$sR$）赋予该概念以**客观现实**的地位，指的是“真实的社会情境”，即作者假设存在的一种真实事态，即便处于该情境中的人们可能并未意识到它。这种对社会现实的使用类似于马克思主义中的“虚假意识（false consciousness）”概念，即个体未能意识到自己在阶级分化社会中真实的、被压迫的地位。
* 另一种含义（$Sr$）则强调**世界在社会定义层面上的非客观性/构想性**（Berger & Luckmann, 1972）。当人们共同认定某事属实时，即便它并非事实，人们的行为也会表现得好像它是事实一样，因此这种经社会达成共识的现实版本，在其对人类行为的后续影响中变为了“真实”。

传播（communication）对于 $sR$ 和 $Sr$ 这两个概念都至关重要，尽管它们承载着不同的含义，但最好将它们理解为彼此交织在一起的。双词概念往往如此；这种模糊性成为了更大概念的一部分，正如“大众媒体（mass media）”和“公共舆论（public opinion）”等术语一样。如果一项研究想要充分展现一个复杂思想的内涵，梳理拆解（unpack）这些多重含义是不可或缺的。

同一术语的不同含义通常源于不同的文献体系。这在传播学中相当普遍，因为传播学并不总是一个严密统一的知识体系。有时传播研究是一个“集散地”（Schramm, 1963），是若干社会科学与行为科学，以及文学和论辩传统作者们的交汇重叠之处。

“依赖/依存（dependency）”就是传播学文献中含义因人而异的典型术语：

1. 社会学家 Ball-Rokeach 与 DeFleur (1976) 使用“依赖”来指代一种关于大众媒体效果的宏观视角，他们认为在受众缺乏其他信息来源的议题上，媒体效果将达到最大化；即受众依赖于大众媒体。
2. 其他媒体研究者则测量“电视依赖（television dependency）”，即不阅读印刷媒体的人在获取信息方面专门依赖电视的程度（例如 McLeod & McDonald, 1985）。
3. 在更宏观的层面上，第三世界的批判学者将不平衡的国际传播流视为欠发达国家对西方强国“依附/依赖（dependency）”格局的一部分（Elguea, 1984）。

这三种对“依赖”一词的截然不同的用法虽然不会出现在同一项研究中，但很容易出现在同一本学术期刊的不同页码上。传播学中的文献综述需要关注的是**概念含义**，而不仅仅是**术语本身**。

按**列举法定义（Definition by list）**，指的是找出构成高阶概念的所有低阶概念。概念定义树就是一种有组织的清单；生物学中的林奈分类系统便是一个突出的范例。

“大众媒体（mass media）”这一概念通常是通过列举法而非提炼其核心含义来定义的（因为其核心含义存在歧义：*mass* 是指“大规模生产”，还是指将受众视为“大众/群体”，抑或是两者兼而有之？）。从语法上讲，*media* 是一个复数名词（单数为 *medium*），其基本清单大家耳熟能详，包括报纸、书籍、杂志、电视、广播和电影。大多数时候，这样一个清单就足够了，并且它可以被拆分为树状结构，例如划分为印刷媒体与视听媒体；或者在电视媒体内部，划分为广播电视、录像与有线电视。一位试图估算大众媒体对国民生产总值（GNP）贡献的经济学家（例如 Machlup, 1962）可能会花些时间建立一份更令人满意的清单，但一份简单的清单就能满足大多数学术需求。

当清单上可能出现一个新项目时，定义清单就可能面临“危机”。此时，原本一份得到共识的清单便不再够用，人们开始需要对含义进行提炼。例如在过去，人们并不需要“大众媒体”这个术语。直到大约一个世纪前，“印刷（printing）”这一概念还完全够用；而“大众媒体”作为概念的提出，是为了适应20世纪的新兴事物。在当今的新传播技术时代，它是否依然有用？有线电视和视频租凭似乎很容易被补充进大众媒体清单中；但如果要纳入交互系统（如电子图文 [videotext]、光盘 [videodisc]），则很大程度上取决于人们的定义，而定义又取决于研究目的。例如，电子邮件在大多数清单中都不适用，但在某些特殊目的下可能会被包含进去。如果将其包含在内，就需要一种全新的含义提炼——也许还需要一个“大众媒体”之外的新术语。清单主要是为了方便使用者，但学者之间是相互依赖的，他们的概念决策可能会相互影响。

清单并不仅仅随时间推移而不断膨胀，旧的形式也可能消亡并从清单中消失。例如，像“传单/号外（broadside）”和“新闻纪录片（newsreel）”这样的大众传播形式，今天的清单制定者可能会苦恼于该将它们归于何处，但它们现在已不再具有足够的重要性，不值得再为此费心。清单虽然是一种不完美的概念阐释方法，但往往足够有效。没有必要以一种有组织的方式去思考一个概念可能采取的所有形态，只需要研究其中的一种或几种即可。换句话说，**当一份清单管用时，就让它发挥作用。**

初看起来，按列举法定义似乎比更抽象的提炼法更容易实现，因为提炼法需要对关于某个概念的各种不同论述进行艰苦的阅读和分析。但这两套程序并不是割裂的。在组织一份清单（如概念定义树）的过程中，人们就已经开始制定至少是一套隐性的经验规则。单靠列举法进行定义，即使是一份经过深思熟虑、立得住脚的清单，也孤立地夹在两个重大的局限性之间：

* 其一，它面临着如何解释在建立该清单时遵循了何种规则的问题。实际上，要建立一份清单，我们必须具备某些隐性的属性/特征，也就是说必须进行某种理论分析。因此，在概念阐释之后建立的清单，比仅为了提供例子或进行归类而临时凑数建立的清单要有用得多。
* 其二，清单具有时间局限性，总是容易发生变化。任何给定的清单都局限于眼前的现实。在缺乏进一步定义的情况下，无从得知一项新事物是否“应该”（或“能够”）被添加到清单中。

例如，在美国，“网络晚间新闻”这一概念多年来一直由三家广播电视网（NBC、CBS 和 ABC）的清单所代表，每家在晚餐时间前后都有一档全国新闻节目。后来，有线电视新闻网（CNN）成立，进行24小时全天候的新闻播报。学者面临的一个问题是：“晚间”仅仅是名称中便利的一部分（即名义定义），还是说在傍晚时分呈现新闻具有特殊的学术价值？单凭清单本身无法告诉你它为何包含现有的内容。清单也无法激发想象力。像24小时全天候新闻频道这样的发明，是由**概念化**所引发的——而不是仅仅靠列举既有事物。同样在研究中，一个新的概念可以开辟通往新发现的道路。

**类型学（Typology）** 是一份基于两个或多个属性、按层级组织起来的结构化清单。我们使用某种规则将某种属性应用于分类任务，因此类型学可以被看作是基于多条规则的清单。那么，仅基于一条规则的清单，就是对单一属性的表征，可以被看作是一个**指标（index）**。例如在某些研究中，“大众媒体的使用”由单一指标来表征；而在另一些研究中，它被剖析为基于多个属性构建的类型学。

### **经验定义 (Empirical Definition)**

Hempel (1952) 将概念的经验分析视为自然科学中概念阐释的终极目标。他设想通过经验研究，概念最终能被具体化为操作化术语。例如，“硬度”这一概念就是通过划痕测试来操作化的：如果一种材料能在另一种材料上划出痕迹，那么无论是根据定义还是根据事实，前者都更硬。在人类传播的研究中，并不存在这种硬性固定的定义，未来也不太可能出现。尽管人们对“传播科学”（Berger & Chaffee, 1987）抱有极大热情，但将传播学中的任何概念简化为一组经验指称（empirical referents）的尝试都以挫折告终。人类在行为和含义表达上过于多样化，以至于我们无法为任何重要概念得出单一且完全令人满意的操作化定义。

然而，我们确实拥有某种更为适度的经验定义。**一个概念的经验定义能让我们判断所遇到的某个事件是否属于该概念的一个实例。** 这意味着经验定义告诉我们应该寻找什么，从而做出包含或排除的决定。经验规则规定了研究者所需的条件，使得当他们讨论自己的研究时，其经验证据能够与其表达的含义相吻合。经验指称是概念的一部分，但并不等同于概念本身。正如一个实例只是概念的一部分（反之亦然），经验研究也只是概念性论述体系的一部分（反之亦然）。正如 Blalock (1968) 所言，我们拥有将概念与测量联系起来的**辅助理论（auxiliary theories）**；这些测量理论作为包含它们的实质性理论检验的一部分而被检验。研究也可以专门设计用来检验测量理论本身。经验定义是概念阐释所迈向的目标。其假设是：研究者越能明确其概念在人类经验世界中的含义，他们对其了解就越多，能够向他人贡献的知识也就越多。这一假设已在成千上万个传播研究项目中证明了其价值。

Festinger 对心理学概念“认知失调（cognitive dissonance）”的研究，展示了致力于达成经验定义的范例。他最初对失调的定义相当宽泛，将其描述为当一个人拥有两个认知，“其中一个认知暗示着另一个认知的对立面”时可能被激发的一种痛苦状态（Festinger, 1957）。失调理论曾一度极具生产力，引发了许多与传播相关的实验检验（Brehm & Cohen, 1962）；但在被试尚未采取行动的实验情境中，研究者往往无法发现假设的效果。Festinger 不想放弃这个曾经成效卓著的思想，于是决定在失调与相关概念“认知冲突（cognitive conflict）”之间确立一条界线。因此，他提出了**决定**（或对决定的公开承诺）作为他的新边界（Festinger, 1964）。在做出决定之前，个体经历的是冲突；只有在做出决定之后，同一个人才会体验到失调。尽管这缩减了 Festinger 认知失调概念最初看似拥有的广泛适用范围，但这一边界保留了该概念。失调理论后来被认为适用于比最初设想更窄的行为范围，但在这一缩减后的领域内，该概念依然保持着生命力。这里的重点在于：**关于“决定”的这条规则是一条经验规则。**

能够应用于许多现象的规则具有诸多优势。它们不会像清单那样发生历史性的变迁。它们甚至可以“在理论上”应用于从未发生过的事件，为通过实验创造独特条件提供指导。但经验规则并不容易获得。它们源于持续不断的研究计划，因此在早期严重依赖含义分析。当一条规则最初被构想出来时，它是作为假设被检验的，只有通过了检验，它才有可能成为其他学者采纳的定义性命题。

**必须进行的观察 (Required observations)。** 研究者如何决定何时可以推断某个概念存在？经验定义明确了必须进行的观察。这涉及前面已经涵盖的几个步骤，如单元定义、属性明确、时间点选择或其他必须进行的比较。

正如观察单元可以是个体性的也可以是关系性的（例如，“丈夫”只有相对于妻子时才能被定义），属性也可以被构想为**特征属性（properties）**或**关系属性（relationships）**。传播学中的大多数变量都是以相对方式测量的；例如，说一个人“聪明”或“保守”，通常是指该个体在大群体分布中的位置。不太明显但在概念上更为关键的是，许多传播属性是**关系概念**，而不仅仅是关系测量。例如，要具备“说服力”，一个人必须说服其他人；要成为“顺从者”，一个人必须顺从某种社会规范；在这些情况下，无论是对方还是普遍规范，都是概念本身的一部分，而不仅仅是操作化定义的一部分。在明确必须进行的观察时，含义中的这些组成部分需要在经验定义中予以保留。

表述经验定义问题的另一种方式，是看在推断某个具体情境中是否存在该概念之前，**必须满足的标准**。这包括“必要性”和“充分性”标准，它们与因果推论中的必要条件和充分条件相关。

* **必要条件**是必须满足的标准，否则该概念的存在是不可能的。在经验上证明某事是必要的绝非易事，而且在传播研究中，严格意义上的必要属性极为罕见。
* 更多时候，我们将**充分条件**设定为标准，即若干种观察中的任何一种都可能满足我们对该概念含义的界定。充分性意味着如果满足了该标准，所讨论的概念就会发生，这往往是经验研究中所检验的内容。

概念可以在许多不同的条件下实例化，且每种条件都可能是充分的，但没有一种是必要的。例如，观看频道2的新闻作为“观看电视新闻”这一概念的一个实例是充分的，但不是必要的；观察到一个人转而观看频道5或频道7的新闻，也同样能满足该概念。这就是从由低阶概念构成的清单式含义分析中产生经验定义的例子。

通过提炼法进行的分析通常会导向更高阶的概念。有时概念过于抽象，无法根据必须进行的观察做出令人满意的阐释。例如，我们可能将“关系传播（relational communication）”这一概念定义为需要两个人（A 和 B）以及他们之间的关系（AB）。当 A 的行为被 B 解读为传达了 A 对 AB 关系的感知时，关系传播就发生了。关于对 A 和 B 的观察，需要做出许多概念上的决策：

* A 必须对 AB 关系有感知吗？
* A 发出的信号必须与其感知一致吗？
* B 对该信号的解读也必须一致吗？
* 这必须影响 B 对 AB 关系的感知吗？
* 如果会影响，B 的感知必须朝更接近 A 的方向改变吗？

只有对这些问题中的大多数做出肯定回答，我们才能将关系传播定义为“AB 关系中感知趋同的过程”。但如果我们确实做出了肯定回答，并且严格按照经验定义的想法去做，我们将面临许多、甚至可能是过多的观察工作。经验定义不应给经验研究者带来不可承受的负担。

一些研究者仅使用完整概念的一部分来确定他们必须进行的观察。例如，如果 A 和 B 在随着时间推移对 AB 关系的感知上趋于一致，某位研究者可能就满足于据此推断关系传播已经发生。然而，当这种趋同没有发生时，研究者就会困惑究竟缺失了哪些要素——以及哪些要素原本对该概念是必要的。另一位研究者可能满足于观察关于 AB 关系的显性信息（如“我喜欢你”、“我们沟通得真好”或“让我一个人静静”）。第三位研究者可能希望纳入非语言线索（如着装、身体姿势、眼神接触）。每位研究者都在通过决定观察什么来实质性地改变定义，而每种具体的定义都驱动着一项特定的研究。研究者的经验定义越接近操作化定义（也就是说，高阶术语越是被低阶术语所替代），其概念就会变得越狭隘。在本文的这个例子中，每位研究者都应该意识到，最初的关系传播概念过于宽泛，需要一些限定性术语，以免这些项目中不同的用法混为一谈。

**形式化操作 (Formal operations)。** 这里的“形式化”指的是对经验数据进行的数学运算公式。这些程序是概念操作化定义的一部分，因此应该经过精心设计，以符合概念定义。形式化程序至少包括三个步骤：**测量（measurement）**、**量表化（scaling）** 和 **统计（statistics）**。在每一步中，牢记正在研究的概念都至关重要。形式化操作本身是没有思想的；它们纯粹是公式化的，无法知道研究者试图达成什么目标。测量、量表和统计程序都是工具，但它们本身并不代表概念。研究者有责任选择适合眼前任务的工具。

**测量**包括将符号（通常是数字）赋予观察对象。数字比观察对象本身要容易处理得多。即使是纯粹定性、无序的属性，也会被赋予类别数字（如 $0 = \text{男性}$，$1 = \text{女性}$），以便轻松地转换为百分比，或与其他定性类别相加来创建定量指标。计算机的出现强化了用数字替代定性含义的文员倾向；当人们在浏览计算机输出结果时，极其容易失去对这些含义的关注。

在用一组数字替代一组观察对象时，务必保留概念所需的所有信息。概念阐释应该明确这包括哪些内容；如果没有明确，就需要做更多的工作。在前期概念阐释上投入时间，可以避免因研究者未能预见到需求而遗失关键信息。

传播内容的编码是一项普遍的测量活动，常见于人际互动、大众媒体信息以及调查访谈中开放式回答的研究。概念阐释应该贯穿始终；初步的分类规则会随着研究者在试编码过程中的推进而得到完善。许多传播内容可以以预编码的形式获取，但其他人的代码是为其自身目的设计的，极少能直接适合其他人。Reeves (1989) 注意到，例如电视节目的传统分类（如新闻、娱乐、广告）并不一定对应于影响心理加工过程的变量。

同一组信息可以有多种分类方式，所选的少数几个类别需要经过仔细定义。编码的三条通用原则如下：

* a. **凡事皆有其所**：这通常被称为完全穷尽（collectively exhaustive）类别原则。
* b. **每项仅归一类**：这是互斥（mutually exclusive）类别原则。
* c. **单一分类规则**：每套类别应根据单一分类规则进行定义。

前两条原则通过初步构想类别（部分通过试错）来解决。编码在某种程度上是归纳性的；如果某种类型的项目出现的频率超乎预期，它甚至可能导向一个新的核心变量。重新构想会持续进行，直到编码员对项目的分类达成高度一致。第三条原则是内容编码中最具智力挑战的任务。它并不意味着只能使用一种属性；但它意味着用于构建某种类型学的每种属性都应该被单独阐释。

编码方案不仅需要阐释分类规则（即项目发生变化的维度），还需要阐释类别之间的边界。例如，Butler 与 Paisley (1980) 创建了一个女性主义“意识”量表，用于对大众媒体中的性别歧视进行编码。他们的最低层级被称为“贬低她（Put her down）”；最高层级是“承认她是非刻板印象的（Recognize that she is non-stereotypic）”；介于两者之间的是其他类别：“保持她的传统定位（Keep her in her place）”、“给她双重定位（Give her two places）”以及“承认她是平等的（Acknowledge that she is equal）”。这些类别被视为互斥的，排列在一个顺序量表中；其边界具有实质性的重要意义，而不仅仅是为了方便。

当决定赋予观察对象何种数字时，**量表化**便从测量中演变出来。在经典的量表类型学（Stevens, 1946）中，每一个量表至少是**定名量表（nominal scale）**，也就是说任何测量都为单位所归类的每个类别提供了一个独特的名称（或数字）。随后可以实现更高层级的顺序：**定序量表（ordinal scales）**、**定距量表（equal-interval scales）** 和 **定比量表（ratio scales）**。选择应当建立在概念阐释的基础上，特别是要同时考量潜在的连续体与操作化量表。

在概念阐释中，首先要考虑是将概念视为**连续变量（continuous variable）**还是**离散变量（discrete variable）**。连续变量具有无限系列的数值，个人智力就是一个例子。离散变量是一组本质上不连续的类别，一个无法渐变为另一个。日常生活中两个常见的离散变量是怀孕和死亡。我们甚至有小笑话（如果恰当的话，是概念阐释的一种可接受的补充）来描述这种离散性：一个人不可能“有一点点怀孕”或“轻微死亡”。

尽管我们将某些概念构想为连续变量，但我们的操作化量表没有一个是连续的。测量完全由离散的类别组成，即使某些模拟设备非常接近连续测量。例如，我们习惯于将距离分解为任意的类别，如英寸、英里或光年。这些都是离散的类别；如果潜在变量是连续的，某些在技术上有差异的实例就会被归为一类。

为了精确分类而精细化类别可能是项昂贵的工程，没有必要比研究问题所要求的更精确。量表区间所代表的跨度应当足够精细，以捕捉到研究者预期在所研究现象中有意义的差异。Reeves、Thorson 与 Schleuder (1986) 在测量传播中的时间概念时探讨了这一问题。

只要每一个后续层级都可以被构想为比前一个“更大/更多”，就可以从对连续现象或离散现象的观察中创建**定序量表**。传播学研究中的大多数量表都是定序量表，这使得它们与“变量 X 越大，则变量 Y 越大”这种流行形式的理论表述相兼容。

二分（但属定序）的类别可以组合起来创建扩展的定序量表。例如，“怀孕-未怀孕”和“活着-死亡”这两个二分类别可以结合起来，追踪一个人从母亲怀孕前（0）、胚胎时期（1）、出生至死亡之间的“一生”（2），到死亡后的时间（3）的历史。这些类别是定序的，但间隔显然是不相等的；事实上，两个极端类别（0 和 3）没有外边界。

**定距量表化**包括赋予诸如 0-1-2-3-4-5 的数字，然后在统计分析中将其视为相邻数字之间的距离相等。也就是说，假设 $5-4 = 4-3 = 3-2 = 2-1 = 1-0$。当潜在变量是一个连续体（如时间）时，这通常是恰当的。但这些条件往往无法满足，将定序量表当作定距量表处理往往会产生误差。

定距量表也可以是**定比量表**，这意味着它具有一个**真实的绝对零点**。只有当间隔相等，且零真正意味着该状况完全不存在时，人们才能执行全套数学运算。在形式上，定比量表之所以这样命名，是因为代数上相等的比例在实际上也是相等的。例如，$1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 40/80$，依此类推。在数学上，比例构成了除法；这是另一种说明任何涉及除法形式化操作都需要定比量表的方式。

在传播学研究中，定比量表往往难以立足，因为它们假设了绝对零点以及等距属性。对于我们的许多概念，变化是从“更多”到“更少”，但“零”的概念将难以定义。

**重新量表化（Rescaling）**，即通过数学公式对量表进行转换，是纠正定比量表数值间隔与其概念间隔不对应这一常见方法。**对数转换（Logarithmic transformations）** 常用于此目的。例如，收入的对数比以原始美元表示的收入能更好地代表阶层之间的功能性差异。将两个分别赚取 10,000 美元和 20,000 美元的人之间的差异，与两个赚取 510,000 美元和 520,000 美元的人之间的差异等同起来，可能是荒谬的。但如果我们把美元量表转换为正对数，后者的差异就变得恰当地微不足道了。基于对数（或其他）转换得出的结论应当用恰当的术语表述；相关的分析对原始（原始数据）量表无效。

最早的态度量表化方法之一始于配对比较（二分定序数据），并将其结合以产生等距态度量表 (Thurstone & Chave, 1929)。关于该方法的描述可参考 Edwards (1957)。**多维标度法（Multidimensional scaling）** 在当今传播变量的测量中被广泛使用，它也是从配对比较数据开始的。

有时，研究目的需要将单个定序连续体拆分为两个概念/变量。报纸阅读习惯就是一个例子。有些人从不读报（许多人是因为不会读），而另一些人每天阅读一份或多份；还有一些人偶尔或经常阅读，这些属于中间类别。这显然是一个定序连续体，但取决于我们想要研究什么，它可能代表了两个概念：阅读与否（二分变量）以及阅读量（连续变量）。如果研究目的是分析非读者与读者之间的差异，那么完整量表上的较高类别完全可以合并为一类（无论频率如何，均统称为“读者”）。另一方面，如果研究是为了解释阅读量的多寡，那么在研究中完全排除从不读报的人或许才是明智之举。

统计分析始于描述性统计，例如对变量分布的集中趋势和离散程度的测量。需要描述的内容不仅取决于测量本身，还取决于其概念定义。双变量分析也应当在概念层面有所预判，且统计模型的选择部分受制于所构建的量表类型。在评估操作化定义时如何应用统计分析，将在第 9 章中予以讨论。

诸如测量、量表化和统计技术等形式化操作，其本身并不构成概念的定义。可以肯定地说，没有任何一个统计公式是在设计之初就考虑到了人类传播概念的。概念阐释能够让研究者处于在概念层面决定哪些形式化程序适合该概念的主动地位，而不是试图去寻找可能用来解释某个特定公式结果的含义。

## **5. Levels of Definition**

Hempel (1952) distinguishes three levels of definition in empirical science: nominal definition, meaning analysis, and empirical analysis. These are of progressively greater utility for research in the physical sciences. For communication research, however, meaning analysis is the more central kind of work; empirical definition is an important consideration, but literally to analyze a concept's full meaning in empirical terms, as a physicist might, is highly unlikely.

This outline will follow Hempel's three levels of definition. The reader should not assume, though, that all explication leads eventually to empirical analysis in the sense that a concept can eventually be reduced to a satisfactory operational definition. Definitions in communication study are much more various, and conceived differently within separate theoretical contexts, than is the case with the physical sciences. Still, there is much to be done.

**Nominal Definition**

Names are often assigned to objects (e.g., *The New York Times*, Stephen, or the Sears Tower) and to classes of objects (e.g., child, memo). These nominal labels are perfectly serviceable in everyday conversation, but when applied to the more abstract ideas of communication (e.g., information, understanding, reticence) they are helpful only to the extent that they are accompanied by mutually understood linkages between the label and the object. A nominal definition is an arbitrary name that lacks linking statements; meaning analysis provides that kind of specification. A nominal definition is adopted as a convention; an empirical definition makes distinctions apparent. For example, we may consider *The Wall Street Journal* a newspaper because most of us agree that it is one (nominal definition), or because it meets certain defined criteria that distinguish newspapers from other entities.

Nominal definitions can become problematic when carried beyond their original context. For example, when electronic calculating machines were first built it seemed natural enough to call them computers. Today, though, the term computer doesn't describe very well what these machines do for most of us most of the time. To a young student who is learning to "write on the computer," we may have to explain a bit of history (and quite a bit about the many uses of the computer) so the conventional name is understood. Industrial etymologies account for the persistence of many now-confusing concepts in communication, such as terms from printing (e.g., the press, lower case). Words that once referred to concrete entities have become abstractions whose meanings exist independent of those roots. The basic point here is that giving some object, or idea, a name is not a definition of a concept that will carry its meaning into other contexts.

**Meaning Analysis**

The process of analyzing the meaning of a concept may be approached in two ways: by distillation and by list. In either case, it is useful to organize our thinking into lower-order and higher-order concepts. Lower-order concepts are closer to the world of observation, either in everyday life or in a contrived laboratory setting. A higher-order concept subsumes several lower-order concepts, and the hierarchical organization of this structure of meaning can be imagined as a definitional "tree." The trunk of this tree is the singular concept we are explicating; branching immediately from it are a few higher-order concepts; each of these can branch further into several lower-order concepts; each of these may sprout one or more operational definitions. Meaning analysis is largely an intellectual process, occupying the trunk and major branches of this structure—without totally losing sight of operationalization.

Distillation of the abstract meaning of a concept begins with reading what many different investigators have said about it. This is followed by an intellectual process of boiling the idea down to its essential elements. What, at heart, do various writers mean by the term? The goal might be to find the central meaning of the concept, but this is rarely achieved in practice because few concepts have a single meaning that is agreed upon by all scholars. Instead, the typical product of this kind of analysis is several meanings, each of which is used by some writers.

An example of distillation is provided by Carter's (1962) identification of three different meanings of the concept of stereotyping. He noted that different writers used this term to refer to the polarization, fixedness, or homogenization of attitudes. In his study of an election campaign's effects, he found that only homogenization increased in relation to mass communication.

Many two-word concepts carry two (or more) meanings, but that is not always obvious in discursive writings. An example is social reality. McLeod and Chaffee (12) distilled dozens of writings in which the term social reality was employed, usually without explicit definition. They found two quite different, in some respects opposite, meanings in this literature, which they called social REALITY ($sR$) and SOCIAL reality ($Sr$). The first ($sR$) gives the concept the status of an objective reality, referring to "the real social situation," a true state of affairs the writer assumes to exist even though the people in that situation might not see it. This usage of social reality is akin to the Marxist concept of false consciousness, in which the individual is unaware of his true, oppressed status in a stratified society. The other meaning ($Sr$) emphasizes instead the unreality of the world as it is socially defined (Berger & Luckmann, 1972). When people agree that something is so, even though it is not, they behave as if it were, and so the socially agreed-upon version of reality becomes real in its implications for people's actions. Communication is central to both concepts, $sR$ and $Sr$, and they are probably best understood as intertwined with one another even though they carry different meanings. This is often the case with two-word concepts; the ambiguity becomes part of the larger concept, as in the terms public opinion and mass media. Unpacking these multiple meanings is essential if one's research is to do justice to a complex idea.

Different meanings for the same term usually come from different literatures. This is fairly common in communication, which is not always a coherent body of knowledge. Sometimes communication research is a gathering place (Schramm, 1963), a locus of overlap for several social and behavioral sciences, and for writers in literary and polemic traditions as well.

Dependency is an example of a term that means several different things within the literature of communication. Sociologists Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur (1976) use dependency to refer to a general view of mass media effects, which they suggest will be maximal on topics for which the person has no other communication source; the audience depends on the mass media. Other media researchers measure television dependency, the extent to which people who do not read print media are dependent upon television specifically for their information (e.g., McLeod & McDonald, 1985). At a more macroscopic level, Third World critical scholars see imbalanced international communication flows as part of a pattern of dependency of the less-developed nations on the Western powers (Elguea, 1984). These three distinct usages of the word dependency are not used in the same study, but they can easily show up on different pages of the same journal. Literature reviews in communication need to attend to meanings, not just terminology.

Definition by list consists of identifying all the lower-order concepts that constitute your higher-order concept. The definitional tree is an organized list; the Linnaean classification system in biology is a prominent example.

The concept of mass media is usually defined by list rather than by distillation of its core meaning, which is ambiguous. (Does mass refer to mass production, or to a conception of the audience as a mass, or to both?) Grammatically, media is a plural noun (the singular is medium), and the basic list is fairly well known. It includes newspapers and books and magazines, television, radio, and films. Most of the time such a list suffices, and it can be broken down into a tree form, such as division between print and audio-visual media; or within, say, television, division into broadcast, video, and cable. An economist trying to estimate the contribution the mass media make to Gross National Product (e.g., Machlup, 1962) might spend some time creating a more satisfying list, but a simple one would meet most scholarly needs.

A crisis can occur in a definitional list when a new item appears that might belong on it. A distillation of meaning may be demanded, where an agreed-upon list had sufficed before. At one time, for example, there was no need for the term mass media. The concept of printing sufficed until about a century ago; mass media was created as a concept to accommodate twentieth-century innovations. Is it still useful in our era of new communication technologies? Cable television and video rentals seem easily added to the mass media list. But inclusion of interactive systems (e.g., videotext, videodisc) would depend a lot on one's definition, which in turn depends on its purpose. Electronic mail, for example, would not belong on most lists, but for some purposes it might be included. If it is, a new distillation—and perhaps a term other than mass media—will be needed. A list is mainly a convenience to the user, but scholars are interdependent and their conceptual decisions can affect one another.

A list does not simply expand over time. Old forms may die out and vanish from the list. For instance, such mass communication forms as the broadside and the newsreel, which today's list-makers might ponder where to put, no longer exist in sufficient prominence to be worth the trouble. A list, although an imperfect method of concept explication, is often productive enough. It is not necessary to think in an organized way about all the forms a concept might take, just to study one or a few of them. In other words, when a list will do, let it do.

Definition by list might appear at first blush to be easier to achieve than the more abstract distillation, with its demanding program of reading and analysis of disparate statements about a concept. But the two procedures are not distinct. In the process of organizing a list, such as a definitional tree, one begins to formulate at least an implicit set of empirical rules. Definition by list alone, even if it is a very thoughtful and defensible list, is isolated between two important limitations. Behind it lies the problem of explaining what rules have been followed in building the list. In effect, to make a list we must have some implicit attribute(s), which is to say a theoretical analysis. Thus a list that is built after an explication is much more useful than a list that is simply cooked up for the immediate occasion of providing examples or grouping them.

A second limitation of a list is that it is time-bound, always subject to change. Any given list is limited to the reality of what exists at the moment. In the absence of further definition, there is no way of knowing whether an innovation should (or could) be added to the list. For instance, the concept of network evening news in the United States was for some years represented by a list of three broadcast networks: NBC, CBS, and ABC, each with a national news program at approximately the dinner hour. Then Cable News Network was formed, transmitting news around the clock. One question for the scholar is whether evening has been merely a convenient part of the name (i.e., a nominal definition) or if presentation of news in the early evening is of particular interest. The list alone does not tell you why it includes what it does. Nor does a list inspire the imagination. Inventions, such as an around-the-clock news channel, are evoked by conceptualization—not by a mere listing of what already exists. Likewise in research, a new concept can open the path to discovery.

A typology is an organized list based on two or more attributes, hierarchically ordered. A rule is used to apply an attribute to the task of classification, so a typology can be thought of as a list based on several rules. A list based on only one rule, then, consists of the representations of a single attribute, and can be thought of as an index. In some studies mass media use, for example, is represented by a single index; while in others, it is analyzed as a typology built on several attributes.

**Empirical Definition**

Hempel (1952) considered empirical analysis of a concept the ultimate goal of explication in the physical sciences. He envisioned the formation of concepts that, through empirical research, would eventually be specified in operational terms. The concept of hardness, for example, was operationalized by the scratch test: If one material can cut a scratch in another, it is harder—both by definition and in fact. No such hard-and-fast definitions are at hand in the study of human communication, nor are they likely to be. Despite considerable enthusiasm for "communication science" (Berger & Chaffee, 1987), attempts to reduce any concept in communication to a set of empirical referents have met with frustration. Human beings are too various, in their behaviors and their meanings, for us to arrive at a single fully satisfactory operationalization of any important concept.

We do, however, have empirical definitions of a more modest kind. An empirical definition of a concept enables us to conclude whether an event we encounter is an instance of that concept or not. This means that the definition tells us what to look for to make decisions about both inclusion and exclusion. Empirical rules specify conditions that researchers require so that when they discuss their research, their evidence coincides with their meaning. The empirical referents are part of the concept, but not identical with it. Just as an instance is only part of a concept (and vice versa), empirical research is only part of a body of conceptual discourse (and vice versa). As Blalock (1968) puts it, we have auxiliary theories linking our concepts with our measures; these measurement theories are tested as part of the test of a substantive theory that includes them. Research may also be designed to test the measurement theory itself. Empirical definition is a goal toward which explication works. The assumption is that the more clearly researchers can specify what their concept means in the world of human experience, the more they know about it and the more knowledge they can contribute to others. This assumption has proven itself worthwhile in a thousand and more programs of communication research.

An example of research working toward an empirical definition is found in Festinger's studies on the psychological concept of cognitive dissonance. He originally defined dissonance quite broadly, as a noxious state that can be aroused in a person who holds two cognitions "one of which implies the obverse of the other" (Festinger, 1957). Dissonance theory became for a time highly productive, generating many experimental tests related to communication (Brehm & Cohen, 1962); but in experimental situations where the subjects had not yet acted, researchers often failed to find hypothesized effects. Festinger, not wanting to abandon an idea that had served well, decided that a demarcation was needed between dissonance and a related concept, cognitive conflict. So he proposed decision (or public commitment to a decision) as his new boundary (Festinger, 1964). Up to the point of decision, the person can experience conflict; only after a decision is made would the same person experience dissonance. Although it cut back Festinger's concept of cognitive dissonance from the broader sweep it had once seemed to offer, this boundary preserved it. Dissonance theory came to be viewed as applicable to a narrower range of behavior than originally envisioned, but within this reduced domain the concept remained viable. The point here is that the rule concerning decision was an empirical one.

Rules that can be applied to many phenomena have a number of advantages. They do not change historically the way lists do. They can even be applied "in theory" to events that have never occurred, providing a guide to experimental creation of unique conditions. But empirical rules are not easy to come by. They grow out of a sustained program of research, so early on they are heavily dependent upon meaning analysis. When first conceived, a rule is tested as a hypothesis, and only if it passes its test is it likely to become a definitional proposition that other scholars will adopt.

*Required observations.* How does a researcher decide when to infer that the concept exists? An empirical definition specifies the observations that are required. This involves several steps already covered here, such as unit definition, specification of attributes, and time points or other comparisons that must be made.

Just as units of observation may be either individual or relational (e.g., "husbands" are defined only in relation to wives), attributes may be conceived as either properties or relationships. Most variables in communication are measured in a relative fashion; to say that a person is "intelligent," or "conservative," for example, usually describes that individual's location in a distribution for a large population. What is less obvious, and more critical conceptually, is that many communication attributes are relationship concepts, not just relational measures. For example, to be "persuasive" a person must persuade someone else; to be a "conformist" one must conform to some social norm; in these cases, either the other person or the general norm is part of the concept, not just the operational definition. In specifying required observations, these components of the meaning need to be retained in the empirical definition.

One way of phrasing the problem of empirical definition is in terms of the criteria that must be met before we would infer that the concept exists in a particular case. These include criteria of both necessity and sufficiency, which are related to the terms necessary and sufficient conditions in causal inference.

A necessity is a criterion that must be met, or else the existence of the concept is impossible. Showing empirically that something is necessary is no simple matter, and attributes that are literally necessary are rare in communication study. More often, we set sufficiency as our criterion, in that any of several observations might satisfy our meaning of the concept. Sufficiency implies that the concept in question does occur if the criterion is met, which is often what is tested in empirical research.

There may be a number of different conditions under which the concept is instantiated, and each may be sufficient. No one of them is necessary. For example, viewing Channel 2 news is sufficient as an instance of the concept "viewing of TV news" but it is not a necessity; observing that a person instead views news on Channel 5 or Channel 7 would satisfy the concept just as well. This is an example of the kind of empirical definition that emerges from a meaning analysis that consists of a list of lower-order concepts.

Analysis by distillation generally leads toward higher-order concepts. Sometimes the concept is overly abstract and cannot be satisfactorily explicated in terms of required observations. For example, we might define the concept relational communication as requiring two persons (A and B), and a relationship (AB) between them. Relational communication occurs when A acts in a way that B interprets as signaling what A's perception of the AB relationship is. Many conceptual decisions need to be made about the observation of A and B. Must A have a perception of the AB relationship? Must A's signal match it? Must B's interpretation of it match as well? Must this affect B's perception of the AB relationship? If so, must B's perception change in a direction closer to A's? Only if most of these questions are answered positively could we define relational communication as a process of convergence of perceptions in an AB relationship. But if we do answer them positively, and if we take literally the idea of empirical definition, we would be faced with many, probably too many, observations to make. An empirical definition should not place an impossible burden on the empirical investigator.

Some researchers use only part of a full concept to identify their required observations. For example, one might be satisfied to infer that relational communication has occurred if A and B converge over time in their perceptions of the AB relationship. When this convergence does not occur, though, the researcher is left to wonder which elements were missing—and which were necessary to the concept in the first place. A different researcher might instead be satisfied with observation of overt messages about the AB relationship (e.g., "I like you," "We're really communicating," or, "Leave me alone"). A third might want to include nonverbal cues (e.g., dress, body position, eye contact). Each researcher is changing the definition in substantial ways by deciding what is to be observed, and each specific definition drives a particular study. The closer a researcher's empirical definition gets to operational definition, which is to say the more that a higher-order term is replaced with a lower-order term, the narrower his/her concept becomes. At this point in this example, each researcher should sense that the starting concept of relational communication is too broad, and some qualifying terminology is needed so that the different usages in these projects are not confused.

*Formal operations.* The term formal refers here to formulas for mathematical operations that are performed on empirical data. These procedures are part of the operational definition of a concept, so they should be carefully designed to conform to the conceptual definition. Formal procedures include at least three steps: measurement, scaling, and statistics. At each step, it is important to keep in mind the concept that is under study. Formal operations are themselves mindless; they are purely formulaic and can have no idea what the researcher is trying to accomplish. Measures, scales, and statistical procedures are tools, but they do not in themselves represent concepts. The researcher is responsible for selecting those tools that suit the task at hand.

Measurement consists of the assignment of symbols, typically numbers, to observations. Numbers are much easier to work with than are the observations themselves. Even purely qualitative, unordered attributes are assigned categorical numbers (e.g., $0 = \text{male}$, $1 = \text{female}$) so that they can easily be converted to percentages, or added to other qualitative categories to create quantitative indices. The advent of computers has strengthened the clerical tendency to replace qualitative meanings with numbers; it is all too easy to lose track of these meanings when one is scanning computer output.

In replacing a set of observations with a set of numbers, be sure to retain all the information that the concept requires. The explication should specify what this includes; if it does not, it needs more work. Spending time "up front" on explication can help avoid losing key information because the researcher failed to anticipate the need for it.

Coding of communication content is a popular measurement activity that is common to the study of interpersonal interactions, mass media messages, and open-ended responses in survey interviews, for example. Explication should be an ongoing process throughout; preliminary classification rules become refined as a researcher progresses through practice coding. Much communication content is available in precoded form, but other people's codes have been designed for their purposes and will rarely fit someone else's. Reeves (1989) notes that, for example, traditional categories of television programs (e.g., news, entertainment, advertising) do not necessarily correspond to the variables that affect psychological processing.

A single set of messages can be classified in many ways, and the few categories chosen need to be defined carefully. The three general principles for coding are as follows:

- a. There should be a place for everything; this is often called the principle of collectively exhaustive categories.
- b. There should be only one category for each unit; this is the principle of mutually exclusive categories.
- c. Each set of categories should be defined according to a single classificatory rule.

The first two principles are addressed by preliminary formulation of categories, partly through trial and error. Coding is to some extent inductive; if a type of item occurs with unanticipated frequency, it may even lead to a new focal variable. Reformulation proceeds until coders largely agree on the classification of items. The third principle is the most intellectual task in content coding. It does not mean that only one attribute can be used; but it does mean that each attribute used to form a typology should be separately explicated.

A coding scheme requires explication not only of the rules for classification (i.e., the dimension along which items vary) but also of the boundaries between categories. For example, Butler and Paisley (1980) created a feminist "consciousness" scale for coding sexism in the mass media. Their lowest level is called "Put her down"; the highest is "Recognize that she is non-stereotypic"; in between are other categories: "Keep her in her place," "Give her two places," and "Acknowledge that she is equal." These categories are treated as mutually exclusive, arrayed in an ordinal scale; the boundaries are substantively significant, not merely convenient.

Scaling evolves from measurement when decisions are made about the numbers to assign to observations. In the classic typology of scales (Stevens, 1946), every scale is at least nominal, which is to say any measure gives a unique name (or number) to each category into which units are classified. Successively higher forms of order are possible: ordinal scales, equal-interval scales, and ratio scales. The choice should be based on explication, particularly a simultaneous consideration of the latent continuum and the operational scale.

In explication consider first whether to treat the concept as either a continuous or a discrete variable. A continuous variable has an infinite series of values; personal intelligence is an example. A discrete variable is a set of categories that are inherently discontinuous; one cannot shade into the other. Two common discrete variables in everyday life are pregnancy and death. We even have little jokes (an acceptable adjunct to explication, if apt) to describe the discreteness; one cannot be "a little bit pregnant" or "slightly dead."

Although we think of some concepts as continuous variables, none of our operational scales can be. Measures consist entirely of discrete categories, even though some analog devices come close to continuous measurement. We conventionally break down distance, for example, into arbitrary categories, such as inches, miles, or light-years. These are discrete categories; if the latent variable is continuous, some instances will be classified together that are technically different.

Refining categories for exact classification can be a costly effort, and there is no point in being more precise than the research problem requires. The span represented by a scale interval should be fine enough to detect differences the researcher expects to be meaningful in the phenomena being studied. Reeves, Thorson, and Schleuder (1986) considered this issue in the measurement of time concepts in communication.

An ordinal scale may be created from observations of either continuous or discrete phenomena, as long as each succeeding level can be conceived as "greater than" the last. Most scales in communication research are ordinal, which makes them compatible with theoretical statements of the popular form, "The greater variable X, then the greater variable Y."

Dichotomous (but ordinal) categories can be combined to create a more extensive ordinal scale. For example, the two dichotomies "pregnant-nonpregnant" and "alive-dead" could be combined to track the history of one man from before the time his mother became pregnant (0), to when he was a fetus (1), to the "lifetime" between his birth and his death (2), to the time after he died (3). These categories are ordinal, but the intervals are clearly not equal; indeed, the two extreme categories (0 and 3) have no outer boundaries.

Equal-interval scaling consists of assigning numbers such as 0-1-2-3-4-5 and then treating them in statistical analysis as if the distances between adjacent numbers were equal. That is, it is assumed that $5-4 = 4-3 = 3-2 = 2-1 = 1-0$. When the underlying variable is a continuum (e.g., time) this is usually appropriate. But those conditions are not often met, and treating an ordinal scale as an equal-interval scale often creates error.

An equal-interval scale may also be a ratio scale, which means that it has a true zero point. Only when intervals are equal, and where zero really means the total absence of the condition, can one perform the full range of mathematical operations. Formally, a ratio scale is so called because ratios that appear algebraically equal are in fact equal. That is, for example, $1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 40/80$, and so on. Mathematically, a ratio constitutes division; this is another way of saying that any formal operation that involves division requires a ratio scale.

Ratio scales are often hard to justify in communication research because they assume a zero point as well as equal-interval properties. For many of our concepts the variation is from "more" to "less" but the notion of "zero" would be hard to define.

Rescaling, or transformation of a scale by a mathematical formula, is one method of correcting for the common problem of a ratio scale whose numerical intervals do not correspond to its conceptual intervals. Logarithmic transformations are often used for this purpose. The log of income, for instance, is a better representation of functional differences between strata than is income expressed in raw dollars. It can be ludicrous to equate the difference between two people who make $10,000 and $20,000, respectively, with the difference between two who make $510,000 and $520,000. But if we convert the dollar scale to a positive logarithm, the latter difference becomes properly trivial. Conclusions based on logarithmic (or other) transformations should be couched in appropriate terms; the analyses involved are not valid for the original (raw data) scale.

One of the earliest attitudinal scaling methods started with paired comparisons (dichotomous ordinal data) and combined them to yield an equal-interval attitude scale (Thurstone & Chave, 1929). For a description of this method, consult Edwards (1957). Multidimensional scaling, which is extensively used in the measurement of communication variables today, also begins with paired comparisons data.

Sometimes the research purpose calls for breaking down a single ordinal continuum into two variables. Newspaper reading habits provide an example. Some people never read a newspaper (many because they cannot), while others read one or more every day; still others read occasionally or often, which are intermediate categories. This is certainly an ordinal continuum, but depending on what we want to study, it may represent two concepts: reading (dichotomous), and amount of reading (continuous). If the purpose is to analyze differences between nonreaders and readers, the higher categories on the full scale might well be merged into one (all "readers," regardless of frequency). If, on the other hand, the search is for explanations of the amount of reading, it might be advisable to eliminate people who never read a newspaper from your study entirely.

Statistical analysis begins with descriptive statistics such as those of central tendency and dispersion of the variable's distribution. What is to be described depends not only on the measure itself, but also on its conceptual definition. Bivariate analyses too should be anticipated conceptually, and selection of a statistical model is governed partly by the kind of scale created. Use of statistical analyses in evaluating an operational definition is discussed in Chapter 9.

Formal operations, such as measurement, scaling, and statistical techniques, do not constitute definitions of concepts in themselves. It is safe to assume that no statistical formula was ever created with a concept of human communication in mind. Explication puts the researcher in the position of determining conceptually what formal procedures are appropriate for the concept, rather than attempting to find meanings that might express the results of a particular formula.
