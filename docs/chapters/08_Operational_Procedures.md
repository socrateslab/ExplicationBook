## **8. 操作化程序 (Operational Procedures)**

在任何研究项目中，决定在经验层面做什么是至关重要的关口；如果我们要把这项工作做好，这也会是一个让人极不安稳的时期。我们面临着重大的决策。研究方法教科书通常将这些决策框定为在几大类主要方法之间的选择，例如内容分析法、抽样调查法或实验室实验法。但这些只是进行观察的场所，而我们首先需要知道的是**我们试图观察什么**。这正是我们进行概念阐释的目的所在。方法的选择应当源于我们所达成的概念定义。

操作化定义应当优先于关于操作化程序的其他决策。原则上，我们寻求研究与我们的概念最为契合的人群和情境，而不是反过来。在实践中，这一阶段存在着大量的妥协折中。我们研究传播是因为我们对其中的人和情境感兴趣，而且某些人和情境比其他的更能引发我们的兴趣。但是，“因为我们的概念特别适合大学生而研究他们”与“因为大学生极易接触到而研究他们”，这两者之间有着本质的区别。至少，我们必须注意，我们所选择用于研究的现实世界情境，不能限制我们在操作化定义中试图捕捉的变异性（variation）。

本书并不打算全面或系统地涵盖研究方法。关于研究设计、数据收集、统计分析以及实证研究其他特征的著作已有许多。在此，我们仅在这些事项涉及概念阐释时对其加以讨论。参考文献将仅限于传统传播学研究中相当标准的方法。希望对其他方法感兴趣的读者能将概念层面的关切带入到这些领域中。

#### **被动观察与主动观察 (Passive Versus Active Observation)**

首先需要考量的一个首要问题是：我们的概念阐释所要求的观察是否已经自然发生？如果没有，我们可能不得不使其发生，即**创造这些观察**。传播往往被构想为某种使其他状况发生的方式；如果那种状况的发生不易被观察到，研究者可能不得不创造新颖的信息传播，或者强加对特定、筛选过的信息的暴露，以使该状况在目标人群身上发生。这种程序在关于传播效果的实验研究中非常普遍。这一原则适用于根据概念阐释所要求的任何状况。研究者需要面对的通用问题是：是采取**被动观察者**的角色，还是**主动参与**创造观察所必需的条件？

传播研究中的大量工作都包含这种延伸意义上的主动观察。例如，数据库/档案库（archives）就是为了研究目的而专门创建的。成堆的报纸和私人收集的录像带到处都是。然而，要创建一个适合进行系统性内容分析的数据库，不仅昂贵、耗时耗力且占用空间，这是一项只有极少数研究型图书馆才会投入其中的持续性活动。

记录保存是数据创造的另一种常见形式。人们每天进行成千上万次（取决于具体概念阐释）的传播活动，但无论是他们自己还是其他人，都不会为这些事件保存多少记录。事实上，保存详尽的记录可能会束缚传播行为，使其作为自然事件的表征显得极不逼真。Csikszentmihalyi 与 Kubey (1981) 设计了一种随机抽样人们全天传播行为的方法：每次通过无线“寻呼机”向被试发出信号，要求其填写一份简短的自陈量表。

实验研究建立在主动创造的数据之上，至少表现为对操纵（自）变量的形式。事件被促使发生，以便研究者能够确信观察到它们。被试可以暴露于实验室之外并不存在的信息中，并且可以诱导出同样不可能在其他任何地方发生的反应。例如，Bandura、Ross 与 Ross (1961) 比较了儿童在观看真人或录像中成年“榜样”攻击（拳打、脚踢）充气傀儡后各自的游玩行为。随后在测试情境中，儿童的玩具中包含了一个类似的充气傀儡；操作化的核心问题是儿童是否会模仿榜样的攻击行为。如果不是为了这项研究，这些拍摄下来或未被拍摄的打打闹闹根本就不会发生，尽管该实验的一个潜在初衷在于现实生活中有些儿童确实有太多的打闹行为。

调查研究者可能会依赖受访者在一段时间后回忆传播事件的能力。调查访谈员通常借助详细的提问格式、补充日记以及其他辅助设备来帮助受访者，使其成功充当研究中的自我观察参与者。

就“被动-主动”观察连续体而言，调查研究是一种中间方法。也就是说，调查往往混合了这两种数据。如果不在研究中，许多访谈问题受访者根本不会想到；除非研究者给出一个否定提示（如“关于这一点，您是否有明确意见？”），否则四分之一的受访者甚至会对虚构的事物发表意见（Bishop, Oldendick, Tuchfarber, & Bennett, 1980）。研究者冒着将**创造的数据**误认为是**观察到的事件**的风险；从技术层面来说，唯一被观察到的仅仅是：该受访者被问到了某个特定的问题，并在那个场合给出了某种特定的回答。

主动创造的数据的存在性状态需要予以审慎考量。主动观察的方法是工具，而它们的创造——表现为实验室程序、访谈方法、参与观察甚至探索性概念——是任何科学的核心活动。方法的选择是为了促进观察；所发生的一切既不比没有研究者特殊干预而“自然”发生的事件更真实，也不比它们更虚幻。一位主动的研究者只是在以一种能够做出艰难观察的方式进行操作。数据源于旨在了解我们的世界及其中人类的创造性努力。这种努力与任何其他事件一样自然，但切记：**研究者的行为是该概念操作化定义的一部分。** 当我们想知道如果某些先前未被观察到的条件具备时可能会发生什么，我们可能需要创造出在其他情况下可能不会自行呈现的条件。预试验、可行性研究和形成性研究就是典型的例子。如果结果令人满意，这类实验性研究往往会导向大规模的创新。《芝麻街》（*Sesame Street*）这一极为成功的学龄前教育电视系列剧，在正式向家庭客厅这一“现实世界”播放之前，曾在多种实验格式中经过了极其艰苦的前测（Palmer, 1981）。

在“观察自然发生的事件”与“观察创造出的事件”之间的选择，主要由研究者概念的定义所决定，但在一定程度上也是智力风格的问题。一些研究者倾向于通过将自己局限于被动观察那些无论如何都会发生的事件，来最大化与日常世界的联系。内容分析学者正是这样看待其工作的，那些寻求传播行为无反应测量（unobtrusive measures）的调查研究者也是如此（Webb, Campbell, Schwartz, & Sechrest, 1966）。另一些研究者则更关注将其概念的适用范围扩展到他们认为具有理论重要性、但不太常见的事件上。一些极具原创性的实验学家符合这种描述，许多传播科学家也是如此——他们在阐释了超出日常发生范围的概念后，构想出独特且巧妙的观察方法来寻找这些难以寻觅现象的实例。这两者的界限并非截然分明。例如，一项关于人们如何使用媒体的经典研究，就是围绕“报纸罢工”这一不同寻常的事件展开的；订阅者由此得以体会“失去报纸”对自己究竟意味着什么（Berelson, 1949）。

强调主动或被动观察的每种途径都有其独特的贡献。大多数传播学者倾向于专精于其中一种途径，而不是兼而有之。无论哪种具体方法，都需要大量的学习和实践经验才能掌握。**对于概念阐释而言，核心在于这种选择是审慎而知情的，并且研究者懂得如何以某种方式将概念进行操作化。**

#### **观察单元 (Units of Observation)**

在实地或实验室中选择何种类型的观察单元，应当直接源于研究者对该概念的**单元定义**。遗憾的是，情况并不总是如此。例如，社会系统的属性往往通过个体层面收集的数据来进行操作化；代表心理和社会事件的单元（如决策）也常常被绑定在个体身上。过度依赖个体作为数据收集的焦点（如调查受访者），往往是出于便利。观察（比方说）一位家长或一个孩子，显然比将“父子/母子关系”作为一个单元来进行观察要容易得多。研究者的概念阐释不应在进入实地情境和进行概念操作化时被抛诸脑后。

#### **抽样 (Sampling)**

如果某种概念阐释将概念限制在特定的单元总体（population of units）中，可能就需要特殊的抽样程序。在大众传播学中，概念往往仅适用于特定的总体，例如录像机（VCR）的所有者（Cohen, Levy, & Golden, 1988），或是广播（Herzog, 1944）或电视（Cantor & Pingree, 1983）日间连续剧的受众。对于此类研究，简单随机抽样程序往往并不合适，研究者可能需要构想出诸如分层抽样等目的性抽样方法。

有时我们也会遇到另一种极端——在毫无概念理由的情况下，硬性要求对某个庞大总体（如全美成年人）进行随机抽样。总体样本固然能保证数据集具有相当大的变异性，且将结果外推到可定义的总体有时对于实质性或修辞性目的而言是可取的。但除非概念阐释本身就是专门针对该总体的，否则仅凭概念理由，这种成本高昂的抽样是站不住脚的。意识到这一点后，追求严苛经验概念的传播研究者会将资源集中于数据收集在理论上相关的特征上。

## **8. Operational Procedures**

Deciding what to do empirically is a critical point in any research project, and a very unsettling time if we are doing the job properly. Large decisions face us. Textbooks on research methods typically frame these as choices among major classes of methods, such as content analysis, sample survey, or laboratory experiment. But those are venues for observation, and first we need to know what we are trying to observe. That has been the purpose of our explication. The choice of method should flow from the definition we are reaching.

The operational definition should take precedence over other decisions regarding operational procedures. In principle, we seek to study people and contexts that are most appropriate to our concept, not vice versa. In practice, there is a good deal of compromise at this stage. We study communication because we are interested in the people and the contexts, and some are more interesting to us than others. But there is a difference between studying college students because our concept is particularly suited to them, and because they are easily available to us. At the least, we must take care that the real-world settings we select for study do not constrain the variation we are seeking to capture in our operational definition.

Research methods are not intended to be covered fully or systematically in this book. There are many texts on research design, data collection, statistical analysis, and other features of operational research. Here we will touch on these matters only as they bear upon concept explication. References will be limited to methods that are fairly standard within traditional communication study. The reader interested in other methods will, it is hoped, carry conceptual concerns into those domains.

**Passive Versus Active Observation**

A first-order question to consider is whether the observations our explication requires already occur. If not, we may have to make them occur, that is, create them. Communication is often conceived as a way of making some other condition occur; if that occurrence is not easily observable, the investigator may have to either create novel communications or impose exposure to specific, selected communications for the people in whom the condition is to occur. This procedure is common in experimental research on communication effects. The principle extends to any condition required according to the concept explication. The general question for the investigator is whether to adopt a passive observer's role, or to take an active part in creating the conditions necessary for observation.

A great deal of work in communication research consists of active observation in this extended sense. Archives, for example, are created for use in research. There are stacks of newspapers and private collections of videotapes all about. To create an archive suitable for systematic content analysis, though, is expensive, laborious, and space-consuming, an ongoing activity to which only a very few research libraries commit themselves.

Record keeping is another common form of data creation. People communicate thousands or millions of times (depending upon the explication) a day, but neither they nor others keep many records of those events. Indeed, to keep a thorough record can constrain the communication behavior so that it would be quite unrealistic as a representation of natural events. Csikszentmihalyi and Kubey (1981) have devised a method of randomly sampling people's communication behavior throughout the day by signaling them with a radio "beeper" each time the person is to fill out a brief self-report form.

Experimentation is built upon actively created data, in the form of the manipulated (independent) variable at least. Events are made to occur so the researcher can be confident of observing them. A subject can be exposed to a message that does not exist outside the experimental laboratory, and responses may be elicited that likewise could not occur anywhere else. For instance, Bandura, Ross, and Ross (1961) compared children's play behaviors after seeing a live or filmed adult "model" attack (punch, kick) an inflatable doll. Later, in a test situation, the child's toys included a similar inflatable doll; the operational issue was whether the children would imitate the model's attack. None of this hitting and kicking, filmed or otherwise, would have occurred were it not for this study, although one underlying reason for the experiment was that in real life some children do all too much hitting and kicking.

The survey researcher may rely on a respondent's ability to recall communication events after some time. Survey interviewers often aid recall with detailed question formats, supplementary diaries, and other devices that help the person succeed as a self-observing participant in the research.

Survey research is an intermediate method with respect to the passive-active observational continuum. That is, surveys often mix both kinds of data. Many interview questions would not occur to respondents who are not in the study; one-quarter of interviewees may give opinions even about fictitious matters unless they are offered a negative cue, such as, "Do you have an opinion on this or not?" (Bishop, Oldendick, Tuchfarber, & Bennett, 1980). The researcher runs the risk of mistaking created data for observed events; all that has been observed, technically at least, is that the person has been asked a certain question and has given a particular response to it on that occasion.

The existential status of actively created data needs to be considered carefully. Active methods for observation are tools, and their creation—in the form of laboratory procedures, interviewing methods, participant observation, and even speculative concepts—is a central activity of any science. Methods are designed to facilitate observation; what happens is no more nor less "real" than are events that occur "naturally" without special action on the part of the investigator. An active researcher is simply operating in a way that enables difficult observations to be made. The data result from creative effort that is directed at learning something about our world and the people in it. Such effort is as natural as other events, but bear in mind that the actions of the investigator are part of the concept's operational definition. When we want to know what might happen if certain previously unobserved conditions obtain, we may need to create conditions that otherwise might not present themselves. Pilot tests, feasibility studies, and formative research are examples. If results are promising, such experimental research often leads to large-scale innovation. *Sesame Street*, the highly successful preschool educational television series, was laboriously pretested in many experimental formats before it was ever broadcast to the "real world" of family living rooms (Palmer, 1981).

The choice between observing naturally occurring events and created events is determined mainly by the definition of the researcher's concept, but to an extent it is also a matter of intellectual style. Some researchers prefer to maximize ties to the everyday world by limiting themselves to passive observations of events they know would occur anyway. Content analysts think of their work this way, as do those survey researchers who seek unobtrusive measures of communication behavior (Webb, Campbell, Schwartz, & Sechrest, 1966). Other investigators are more concerned with extending the range of their concepts to less commonplace occurrences that they see as theoretically significant. Some highly original experimenters fit this description, as do many communication scientists who, having explicated concepts that extend beyond the range of everyday occurrence, contrive unique observational methods to find instances of hard-to-find phenomena. The line is not clear-cut. For example, a classic study of how people use media was built around the unusual event of a newspaper strike; subscribers could then see what "missing the newspaper" meant to them (Berelson, 1949).

Each approach, emphasizing active or passive observation, has its contribution to make. Most students of communication tend to specialize in one approach but not both. The particular methods in either case require a good deal of study and experience to master. What is central to explication is that the choice be made knowingly, and that the researcher know how to operationalize a concept one way or the other.

**Units of Observation**

Selection of the kind of unit to observe in the field or laboratory should flow directly from the researcher's unit definition for the concept. Unfortunately this is not always the case. For example, attributes of social systems are often operationalized via individual-level data collection. Units that represent mental and social events, such as decisions, are often tied to individuals as well. Over-reliance on the individual as the locus of data collection (such as a survey respondent) is often due to convenience. It is easier to observe, say, a parent or a child than a parent-child relationship as a unit. The researcher's explication should not be forgotten upon entering the field situation and operationalizing the concept.

**Sampling**

If an explication limits a concept to a specific population of units, special sampling procedures may be called for. In mass communication, concepts often apply only to particular populations, such as owners of videocassette recorders (Cohen, Levy, & Golden, 1988) or the audience for daytime serials on radio (Herzog, 1944) or television (Cantor & Pingree, 1983). For such studies, simple random sampling procedures are often inappropriate, and the investigator may need to devise purposive methods such as stratified sampling.

Sometimes we encounter the other extreme—demanding a random sample of some very large population (e.g., U.S. adults) for no conceptual reason. A general population sample guarantees there will be considerable variation in the data set, and extrapolation of results to a definable population is sometimes desirable for substantive or rhetorical purposes. But unless the explication is specific to that population, this rather costly kind of sampling is not justifiable on conceptual grounds alone. Realizing this, communication researchers who are pursuing an empirically demanding concept concentrate their resources instead on theoretically relevant features of data collection.
